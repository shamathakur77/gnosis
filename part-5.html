The word itself

Greek has two words for knowing.

This is the place to start, because in English the distinction has collapsed. We say "I know" for both "I know the multiplication tables" and "I know my mother". The Greek language did not collapse them. It kept them separate, because they are not the same thing.

The first kind, the multiplication-tables kind, is called episteme in Greek. Reflective, propositional knowledge. The kind you can write down and test. The kind a teacher can transfer to a student. The kind you can be quizzed on.

The second kind is called gnosis. It is the knowing that comes from direct contact. Knowing your mother, in the sense of having spent years near her and recognising her step in the hallway. Knowing a city by having lived in it. Knowing a piece of music by having heard it sung from the inside. Knowing yourself by being yourself, attentively, for long enough to recognise the texture of what you are.

Elaine Pagels, in her 1979 book that brought the Nag Hammadi material to mainstream readers for the first time, translated this distinction as the difference between knowledge and insight. She wrote, with characteristic precision, that gnosis is not primarily rational. It is an intuitive process of knowing oneself, and to know oneself, at the deepest level, is simultaneously to know God.

This is the entire Gnostic project, in a single sentence.

Two words. Two paths.

The argument was never about Christianity. It was about where authority sits.

The early bishops put their weight on pistis. The Greek word for faith. Pistis is intellectual and emotional acceptance of a proposition. It can be taught, written down, ratified by councils, enforced by hierarchy. It can sustain an institution.

The Gnostic teachers put their weight on gnosis. The Greek word for direct, experiential knowing. Gnosis cannot be taught second-hand. It cannot be ratified by councils. It cannot sustain an institution because it requires no intermediary.

If pistis is the foundation of the church, the church is necessary. If gnosis is the foundation, the church is, at best, optional.

The fight between these two words is the fight that produced the canon, the creeds, the burnings, and the buried jar at Nag Hammadi.

The four questions

The gnostic teacher Theodotus gave a definition.

Theodotus was a Valentinian Christian teacher in Asia Minor around 140 to 160 CE. His writings survive only in fragments preserved by a hostile witness, Clement of Alexandria, who quoted him in order to argue against him. Even so, one passage made it through. Theodotus said that the gnostic is the one who has come to understand:

Who we were, and what we have become.
Where we were, and into what we have been thrown.
Whither we are hastening, from what we are being redeemed.
What birth is, and what is rebirth.

Read this slowly. It is not theological. It is autobiographical. Theodotus is not asking what doctrines to accept. He is asking what you actually know, in the experiential sense, about your own life. About where you actually came from before you were here. About what you have become in the process of being here. About where you are headed. About what is happening when you arrive, and what is happening when you leave.

These are not abstract questions. They are the questions that come up in the middle of the night. They are the questions that come up when someone you love dies, or is born. They are the questions that the architecture of ordinary life, the 365 angels of the working week, is designed to keep you too busy to ask.

Gnosis, in Theodotus's definition, is the answer that arrives when you ask them and sit still long enough to hear something back.

The Gnostic teacher Monoimus said it even more compactly:

"Abandon the search for God and the creation and other matters of a similar sort. Look for him by taking yourself as the starting point."

Gospel of Thomas · Saying 70
"If you bring forth what is within you,
what you bring forth will save you.
If you do not bring forth what is within you,
what you do not bring forth will destroy you."
Buried 367 CE · Found 1945
A clarification

This is the part where it is easy to lose the plot.

The word gnosis, in 2026, gets used to sell things. Crystals. Online courses. Manifestation programmes. There is a whole modern industry built on the suggestion that buying the right product will deliver direct inner knowing.

The ancient Gnostics would not have understood this. Not because they were against beauty, or commerce, or aesthetics. The Valentinians were sophisticated city dwellers, not desert ascetics. The point is that gnosis is precisely the thing that cannot be sold to you by someone else, because the moment it is delivered second-hand, it becomes pistis. Belief in someone else's report. Which is the opposite of what gnosis means.

This is also why the Gnostic teachers spent so much time arguing with their own students. The teacher's role was not to give you gnosis. The teacher could only point. Could only tell stories, like the Sophia myth, that pointed past themselves toward something the student would have to recognise alone.

If a teacher tells you they will give you gnosis, run.

If a teacher tells you that gnosis is the thing inside you that already knows, and that their job is just to help you stop interrupting it, listen carefully.

A practice, not a programme

What you can actually do this week.

The texts do not give you a six-step plan. They do not have an evening course you can buy. They are mythological and poetic, not instructional. But there is something that consistently shows up across the different Nag Hammadi texts as the actual practice. It is small. It is repeatable. It is free.

One. Notice the architecture.

This week, when you feel yourself pulled toward something that does not actually serve you, the comparison spiral, the doom scroll, the work obligation that has become an identity, the fear that is not actually yours, name it. Not as evil. As architecture. As one of the seven, doing its job. Notice it. You do not have to fight it. Naming it is most of the move.

Two. Ask the four questions.

Theodotus's four questions are a meditation. Who was I before this. What have I become inside it. Where am I going. What is the difference between this birth I am having and the rebirth Theodotus is talking about. You do not need to answer. You need to sit with them.

Three. Find one moment of unmediated knowing.

One moment in the day where you know something directly, not because anyone told you, not because you read it, not because the algorithm served it to you. The taste of cold water. The exact colour of the sky right now. The fact that you love a particular person. The fact that something in your work is wrong and you have known it for months. The fact that something in your work is right and you have known that for months too.

The Gnostic texts say that gnosis is built from these moments. The cumulative recognition that you are not actually as cut off from direct knowing as the architecture wants you to believe.

Four. Refuse to be the mediator.

If you teach, or write, or lead, or parent, the Gnostic instruction is to point past yourself. To refuse to be the place where the student stops. To make yourself, in the technical sense, useless as soon as possible. Your job is not to be believed. Your job is to be the temporary scaffold for someone else's direct knowing.

This is the hardest of the four. Almost every institution we work inside, including the ones we build ourselves, will reward the opposite.

Gospel of Thomas · Saying 113
"The kingdom of the Father is spread out upon the earth, and people do not see it."
The final saying. Buried 367 CE · Found 1945.
A closing

You read this far.

That, by itself, is a small piece of evidence. The architecture of an ordinary week does not naturally make space for five long-form essays on a buried jar in the Egyptian desert and the books that were inside. Something in you arranged for the space. Something in you wanted to know.

The Gnostic texts would say that the something is the spark. The part of you that came from Sophia's mother and travelled through Sophia and through Yaldabaoth and through the seven archons and through your own birth into the body you are sitting in right now. The part that has never actually forgotten where it came from. It has only been waiting for you to listen to it.

You do not need to convert. You do not need to join anything. You do not need to call yourself a Gnostic. You do not need to buy a crystal or a course or a programme. The texts do not require any of that. They were buried by people who refused to burn them, found by a farmer with a pickaxe, and are now sitting in your browser. That is enough.

What they ask is small and difficult. To take yourself seriously as a starting point. To distinguish between what you believe because you have been told and what you know because you have seen. To carry the four questions with you, lightly, into ordinary life. To recognise the architecture without becoming consumed by hating it.

And, when you find the spark, to bring forth what is within you. Because, the Gospel of Thomas says, that is the thing that will save you. And the thing you do not bring forth is the thing that will not.
